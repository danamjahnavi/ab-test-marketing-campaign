# Executive Summary: Marketing Campaign A/B Test

**Question.** Did showing users our ad (instead of a public service announcement) increase conversions, and is the campaign worth continuing?

**Answer. Yes, the ad increased conversions.** Among 588,101 users, those who saw the ad converted at 2.55%, compared with 1.79% for the randomly chosen control group that saw a public service announcement. That is 43% more conversions (plausible range: 30% to 58% more). The result is very unlikely to be chance, and three independent calculations gave the same answer.

**What it adds up to.** Across the 564,577 users who saw the ad, the campaign produced about **4,300 extra conversions** (plausible range: 3,400 to 5,300) from 14.0 million ad impressions.

**Does it pay?** Our data has no cost or revenue figures, so this depends on two numbers finance must supply. As an **illustration only**: if ads cost $5 per 1,000 impressions, the campaign breaks even when one conversion is worth about **$16** (between $13 and $21 given the uncertainty). Above that value, it makes money.

| Assumed ad cost per 1,000 impressions (illustrative) | Break-even value of one conversion |
|---|---|
| $2 | about $6 |
| $5 | about $16 |
| $10 | about $32 |

**An important pattern.** Two thirds of users saw 20 or fewer ads, and for them the ad made no measurable difference. The extra conversions came from users who saw many ads. This does not prove that showing more ads causes more sales; heavy viewers may simply be more active customers. It does mean a lot of spend may be going where it has no visible effect.

## Recommendation

**Continue the campaign, provided a conversion is worth more than the break-even value at our real ad cost.** At the same time:

1. **Keep a random 10% of users on the PSA** so we can keep measuring whether the ad still works.
2. **Run a follow-up test on ad frequency** (randomly give some users fewer ads and some more) before increasing spend. This tells us whether money should go to reaching more people or to showing ads more often.

## Risks

- **Only 4% of users were in the control group**, and we don't know if that was intended. The estimate is solid but less precise than the total user count suggests.
- **We can't see dates.** We don't know how long the test ran or whether the effect fades over time.
- **No cost or revenue data.** Profit figures are illustrations until finance supplies real values.
- **No check for side effects** such as unsubscribes or complaints.

## What could change the decision

- A conversion worth less than the break-even value at our real ad cost: stop or cut spend.
- Evidence that the effect fades after the first weeks.
- A rise in complaints or unsubscribes.

## Next step

Get the real cost per 1,000 impressions and the value of a conversion from finance, then launch the frequency test with a 10% holdout, a fixed length of at least two weeks, and complaint/unsubscribe tracking.
